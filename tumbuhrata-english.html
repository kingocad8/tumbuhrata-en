Tools, not a consultation

TumbuhRata sells tools — checklists, logs, and planners that help you notice, record, and practice. It does not diagnose, and it never replaces your child's doctor. When something worries you, the app's job is to help you walk into that appointment with a clear, written list.

In the CDC's words: “Learn the Signs. Act Early. materials are not a substitute for standardized, validated developmental screening tools.”

Where does the content come from?

No invented milestones, no opinion pieces. The checklists and tools are built from three public sources — and every item inside the app carries its source label.

CDC · Learn the Signs. Act Early. WHO AAP · HealthyChildren.org
See the full source list
  • Milestones: CDC “Learn the Signs. Act Early.” checklists (revised February 2022 with the AAP) — cdc.gov/act-early/milestones. One page per checkpoint: 12 months, 15 months, 18 months, 2 years, 30 months, 3 years.
  • What to do if you're concerned: CDC, “If You're Concerned” — cdc.gov/act-early/families/concerned.
  • Screening schedule: American Academy of Pediatrics — general developmental screening at 9, 18, and 30 months; autism screening at 18 and 24 months, or whenever there is a concern.
  • Sleep amounts: American Academy of Sleep Medicine sleep-duration consensus, endorsed by the AAP.
  • Tantrum & toilet-readiness guidance: American Academy of Pediatrics parent guidance (HealthyChildren.org).

The CDC defines its milestones plainly: “Developmental milestones are things most children (75% or more) can do by a certain age.”

Is this a diagnosis or a medical screening?
No. TumbuhRata is a set of parent tools built on the CDC's milestone checklists. The CDC itself says its “Learn the Signs. Act Early.” materials are not a substitute for standardized, validated developmental screening tools. The app helps you observe and keep notes — your child's doctor does the diagnosing.
Which milestone standard do you use?
The CDC “Learn the Signs. Act Early.” checklists, revised in February 2022 together with the AAP. Checkpoints are 12, 15, 18, 24, 30, and 36 months, and every item is something 75% or more of children can do by that age. Items are shown in the CDC's own wording.
What if my child isn't meeting a milestone?
The CDC's advice is direct: “You know your child best. If your child is not meeting the milestones for his or her age… talk to your child's doctor and share your concerns. Don't wait. Acting early can make a real difference!” The Results tab turns your answers into a written list you can bring to that conversation, and the AAP recommends formal developmental screening at 9, 18, and 30 months (autism screening at 18 and 24 months) — ask your doctor about it.
What ages does it cover?
Ages 1–3 years. Milestone checkpoints run 12–36 months; Learning activities are split into 1–2 years and 2–3 years so they match what your child can actually do.
